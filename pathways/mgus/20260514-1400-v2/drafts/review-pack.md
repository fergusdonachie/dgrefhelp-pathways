## Page contents

### Paraprotein / monoclonal gammopathy

This pathway is written from the GP's entry point: **the laboratory has reported a paraprotein or monoclonal band — what do I do now?** Most cases will turn out to be MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance), a benign condition, but the same finding can indicate more serious haematological disease. This pathway guides you through workup, risk stratification, and the decision to refer or monitor.

### What is a paraprotein?

A paraprotein (also called an M-protein or monoclonal band) is an abnormal immunoglobulin — or fragment of one — produced by a single clone of plasma cells or B-lymphocytes. It is detected on serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) as a discrete band in the gamma region, and quantified in g/L. The class of the paraprotein (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, or light chain only) is determined by immunofixation.

### When should a myeloma screen be requested?

Consider requesting SPEP + urine Bence Jones protein (BJP) in any patient with:

- Unexplained anaemia (Hb below normal without clear cause)
- Bone pain, especially back pain or rib pain
- Recurrent bacterial infections
- Raised ESR or plasma viscosity
- Hypercalcaemia
- Unexplained renal impairment
- Fatigue and significant weight loss
- Incidental monoclonal band on protein electrophoresis or raised total protein on routine bloods

### What can a paraprotein indicate?

Detection of a paraprotein does not mean myeloma — it represents a spectrum of conditions:

- **MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance)** — the most common finding; a benign condition requiring monitoring but not treatment
- **Smouldering (asymptomatic) myeloma** — higher paraprotein levels but no end-organ damage; intermediate risk of progression
- **Multiple myeloma** — active disease with end-organ damage (CRAB features); requires treatment
- **Waldenström's macroglobulinaemia** — IgM paraprotein associated with lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma
- **AL amyloidosis** — light chain deposition causing organ damage (kidneys, heart, nerves)
- **Other lymphoproliferative disorders** — e.g. CLL, lymphoma, plasma cell leukaemia

### The goal of GP assessment

Identify features of serious underlying disease that require **urgent referral** versus patients who are safe for **community monitoring**. The sections below guide you through this process step by step.

### Primary care assessment

#### Step 1 — Complete the workup (if not already done)

A full myeloma screen should include the following. Many labs now report SPEP and FLC together; check what has been requested and request anything missing.

| Test | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) + immunofixation | Paraprotein type (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, or light chain only) and concentration in g/L |
| Serum free light chains (FLC) | Kappa:lambda ratio. Normal ratio 0.26–1.65. An abnormal ratio increases risk of progression |
| Urine Bence Jones protein | Request early morning urine protein:creatinine ratio (PCR); if raised, request early morning urine for full electrophoresis |
| FBC | Anaemia (Hb <100 g/L triggers urgent referral), leucopenia, thrombocytopenia |
| U&E | Renal impairment (creatinine >177 µmol/L or eGFR <40 ml/min triggers urgent referral) |
| Corrected calcium, albumin, LDH | Hypercalcaemia (>2.75 mmol/L triggers urgent referral) |

#### Step 2 — Look for CRAB features

> **⚠ RED CALLOUT — CRAB features: must not be missed**
>
> Any CRAB feature indicates possible myeloma or a related serious haematological disorder. Arrange **urgent USC (2-week wait) haematology referral** immediately — do not wait to complete further workup.
>
> | Feature | Threshold |
> |---|---|
> | **C**alcium (hypercalcaemia) | Corrected calcium >2.75 mmol/L |
> | **R**enal impairment | Creatinine >177 µmol/L or eGFR <40 ml/min without other cause |
> | **A**naemia | Hb <100 g/L without other cause |
> | **B**one lesions | Lytic lesion, pathological fracture, or severe osteoporosis on imaging |
>
> Also refer urgently for: significant bone pain, recurrent bacterial infections, hyperviscosity symptoms (visual disturbance, headache, confusion), peripheral neuropathy, features of AL amyloidosis (oedema, macroglossia, carpal tunnel syndrome), or any Bence Jones proteinuria with raised urine PCR.

#### Step 3 — Identify paraprotein type and level

The laboratory report will state the immunoglobulin class (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, or light chain only) and paraprotein concentration in g/L. Record both — they are needed to apply referral thresholds and for any future haematology referral.

Note: IgD and IgE paraproteins of _any_ level should be referred to haematology. IgM paraproteins of any level should be discussed with haematology given the risk of Waldenström's macroglobulinaemia.

#### Step 4 — Risk stratify (patients without CRAB features)

For patients without CRAB features and below urgent referral thresholds, apply the Mayo Clinic / International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) model. Three independent risk factors predict progression:

1. M-spike ≥15 g/L
2. Non-IgG paraprotein type (i.e. IgA, IgM, IgD, or light chain only)
3. Abnormal serum free light chain ratio (<0.26 or >1.65)

| Risk factors present | Risk group | Approx. 20-year progression risk | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Low | ~5% | Primary care monitoring |
| 1 | Low-intermediate | ~21% | Primary care monitoring |
| 2 | Intermediate-high | ~37% | Refer to haematology (routine) |
| 3 | High | ~58% | Refer to haematology (routine) |

### Primary care management

For patients with **low or low-intermediate risk MGUS**, no CRAB features, and paraprotein below referral thresholds — ongoing monitoring in primary care is appropriate and safe.

#### Monitoring schedule

| Risk group | First review | Ongoing frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Low (0 risk factors) | SPEP + FBC / U&E / calcium at 6 months | If stable: annually for 2–3 years, then every 2–3 years |
| Low-intermediate (1 risk factor) | SPEP + FBC / U&E / calcium at 6 months | Annually |

Each monitoring visit should include SPEP (to trend paraprotein level), FBC, U&E, and corrected calcium. Serum FLC does not need to be repeated at every visit unless the ratio was borderline or the paraprotein level is rising.

#### Triggers to refer during monitoring

> **⚠ YELLOW CALLOUT — Refer to haematology if any of the following occur during monitoring:**
>
> - Paraprotein increase **>25% from baseline AND absolute increase ≥5 g/L** within 6 months (rapid progression)
> - Any new CRAB feature: hypercalcaemia, worsening renal function, new anaemia (Hb <100 g/L), or new bone lesion
> - New or worsening symptoms: bone pain, recurrent infections, significant weight loss, hyperviscosity symptoms (headache, visual disturbance), peripheral neuropathy
> - Patient request for specialist review
>
> Do not wait for the next scheduled review — refer promptly if any trigger is met.

#### What to tell patients

- **MGUS is not cancer.** It is an abnormality of blood proteins that most people live with without it ever becoming a problem.
- The vast majority of people with MGUS never develop myeloma or any serious illness as a result of it.
- Monitoring blood tests are routine — they are simply to detect any change at an early stage, when it can be acted on promptly.
- Patients should report any new symptoms between scheduled appointments — they should not wait for the next review if they develop bone pain, recurrent infections, or other new concerns.
- MGUS does not need to be treated — there is no medication that prevents progression, and treatment is only started if progression to myeloma occurs.

### Who to refer

#### USC (2-week wait) / same-day emergency haematology referral

> **RED CALLOUT — Refer urgently (USC 2WW or same-day if acutely unwell) for any of the following:**
>
> - Any CRAB feature (hypercalcaemia >2.75 mmol/L; creatinine >177 µmol/L or eGFR <40; Hb <100 g/L; lytic bone lesion or pathological fracture)
> - Significant bone pain (especially axial — back, ribs, sternum)
> - Recurrent bacterial infections suggesting immune paresis
> - Hyperviscosity symptoms: headache, visual disturbance, confusion, epistaxis
> - Any Bence Jones proteinuria with raised urine protein:creatinine ratio
> - Features of AL amyloidosis: peripheral oedema, macroglossia, carpal tunnel syndrome, unexplained weight loss, nephrotic syndrome

#### Urgent haematology referral (non-cancer pathway)

> **RED CALLOUT — Refer urgently (non-cancer pathway, within 2 weeks) for:**
>
> - IgG paraprotein >15 g/L
> - IgA or IgM paraprotein >10 g/L
> - IgD or IgE paraprotein of any level
> - IgM paraprotein of any level — discuss with haematology (risk of Waldenström's macroglobulinaemia)
> - Paraprotein rise >25% from baseline AND absolute increase ≥5 g/L within 6 months
> - Light chain MGUS — abnormal FLC kappa:lambda ratio with no intact paraprotein on SPEP
> - Diagnostic uncertainty or any clinical concern not covered above

#### Routine haematology referral

> **BLUE CALLOUT — Refer routinely (non-urgent) for:**
>
> - 2 or 3 Mayo risk factors (intermediate-high or high risk MGUS)
> - Patient request for specialist reassurance or review

#### What to include in all haematology referrals

> **BLUE CALLOUT — Include in all referrals:**
>
> - SPEP result: paraprotein type and concentration in g/L (and date)
> - Serum free light chain result: kappa and lambda values, kappa:lambda ratio
> - FBC, U&E, LFTs, corrected calcium, albumin, LDH
> - Urine Bence Jones protein result (or urine PCR)
> - Previous SPEP results where available — trend over time is clinically important
> - Presence or absence of each CRAB feature
> - Current symptoms (bone pain, infections, neuropathy, weight loss etc.)
> - Relevant comorbidities and medications

### Who not to refer

- **Low or low-intermediate risk, asymptomatic, stable** — IgG paraprotein below referral threshold (<15 g/L), normal or only mildly abnormal FLC ratio, no CRAB features, no symptoms. Primary care monitoring is appropriate and recommended.
- **Do not refer solely because a paraprotein has been detected** — complete the full workup (Step 1) and apply risk stratification criteria before making a referral decision. Incomplete workup leads to uninformative referrals.
- **Very elderly or frail patients without symptoms** — community monitoring is appropriate. If uncertain about management in this group, contact haematology by email or telephone for advice before referring rather than arranging a formal outpatient appointment that may not benefit the patient.
- **Patients not fit for or not wishing treatment** — community monitoring with clear documentation of the patient's wishes is appropriate. Seek haematology advice by phone or email if clinical management is uncertain.
