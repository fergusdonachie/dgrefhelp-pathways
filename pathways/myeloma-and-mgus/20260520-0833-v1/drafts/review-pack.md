# Myeloma and MGUS

## Page contents (Background)

MGUS is common, usually asymptomatic, and not cancer. It is often found incidentally and does not need treatment, but it requires risk-based monitoring because a minority of patients progress to myeloma or related lymphoplasmacytic malignancy.

The key primary-care task is to identify myeloma early, recognise CRAB features, and arrange same-day assessment when malignant spinal cord compression or acute kidney injury is suspected.

## Accordion: Assessment

### History

- Ask about unexplained bony back pain or other bone pain.
- Ask about unexplained fracture, fatigue, recurrent infection, polyuria, polydipsia, confusion, pruritus without rash, neuropathy, weight loss, and symptoms of anaemia.
- In known MGUS, ask about new or worsening bone pain, fractures, fatigue, recurrent infections, neuropathy, weight loss, and renal symptoms.

### Examination

- Assess hydration, pallor, bone tenderness, and neurological status.
- If back pain is present, check gait, power, sensation, reflexes, and bladder or bowel disturbance.

### Tests

- Initial tests for possible myeloma: FBC, U&E/eGFR, calcium, ESR or plasma viscosity, LFT, serum immunoglobulins, serum protein electrophoresis, and serum free light chains.
- Request urine Bence Jones protein if serum free light chain testing is not available.
- If serum protein electrophoresis is abnormal, serum immunofixation is needed to confirm paraprotein.
- Do not rely on one negative paraprotein, light-chain, or urine test alone to exclude myeloma if symptoms remain concerning.
- If tests are normal but symptoms remain unexplained and concerning, repeat baseline tests and safety-net; consider specialist advice or an alternative diagnostic route.

### Red flags

- Suspected myeloma with malignant spinal cord compression symptoms/signs: same-day hospital assessment.
- Blood tests or imaging suggestive of myeloma with acute kidney injury: same-day hospital assessment.
- Myeloma-suggestive tests plus any CRAB feature: urgent suspicion of cancer referral.

## Accordion: Primary care management

- Treat MGUS as a monitoring diagnosis, not a treatment diagnosis.
- Give clear safety-net advice to report new bone pain, fractures, fatigue, recurrent infections, neuropathy, weight loss, polyuria, polydipsia, or symptoms of anaemia.
- Low-risk MGUS means IgG paraprotein <15 g/L, normal serum free light chain ratio, no CRAB features, no concerning symptoms, and stable results.
- For newly identified low-risk MGUS, repeat FBC, U&E/eGFR, calcium, paraprotein/serum protein electrophoresis, quantitative immunoglobulins, and serum free light chains at 3 to 6 months to establish stability.
- If stable, continue annual monitoring unless local haematology advises a longer interval.
- Consider stopping routine surveillance for low-risk MGUS in people over 80 years or with life expectancy under 5 years after shared decision-making and local haematology agreement.
- Do not start treatment for MGUS in primary care.

## Accordion: Who to refer

### Hospital admission (same day)

- Suspected myeloma with symptoms or signs of malignant spinal cord compression.
- Blood tests or imaging suggestive of myeloma with acute kidney injury.
- Severe hypercalcaemia or acute systemic illness should follow local emergency assessment routes.

### Urgent suspicion of cancer (USOC)

- Raised paraprotein concentration, paraprotein band, abnormal serum free light chains, or urinary Bence Jones protein plus one or more CRAB features:
  - raised calcium
  - renal impairment
  - anaemia
  - bone pain

### Urgent (non-cancer outpatient)

- MGUS with IgG paraprotein >15 g/L.
- MGUS with IgA or IgM paraprotein >10 g/L.
- Serum free light chain ratio outside 0.1 to 7.
- Paraprotein rise >25% and >5 g/L from baseline.
- Any MGUS or paraprotein result with new CRAB symptoms, recurrent infections, neuropathy, suspected AL amyloidosis, or otherwise unexplained clinical deterioration.

### Routine (outpatient)

- Raised paraprotein without CRAB features where MGUS risk thresholds are met or monitoring suitability is uncertain.
- Stable MGUS where local haematology advice is needed about monitoring responsibility or interval.

### Include in referral

- Symptoms and duration, especially bone pain, fractures, infection, neuropathy, weight loss, renal symptoms, and MSCC symptoms if present.
- FBC, U&E/eGFR, calcium, ESR or plasma viscosity, LFT, serum immunoglobulins, serum protein electrophoresis, serum free light chains, urine Bence Jones protein if done, and trend over time.
- Medication list, relevant comorbidity, frailty, and whether the patient is suitable for investigation or active treatment.

## Accordion: Who not to refer

- Do not refer stable low-risk MGUS as USOC when there are no CRAB features, no concerning symptoms, and no significant laboratory change.
- Do not refer isolated raised paraprotein as USOC unless CRAB features or other cancer referral criteria are present.
- Do not treat MGUS in primary care.

## Evidence review

### Source-by-source summary

#### Scottish Referral Guidelines for Suspected Cancer: haematological cancers

URL: https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/scottish-referral-guidelines-srgs-for-suspected-cancer/haematological-cancers/

Covers adult suspected haematological cancer referral in Scotland. It gives the clearest urgency framework for D&G use: same-day emergency referral for myeloma-suggestive tests or imaging with malignant spinal cord compression symptoms/signs or acute kidney injury, and urgent suspicion of cancer referral for myeloma-suggestive results plus CRAB features. It also states that raised paraprotein alone without the additional referral features should not be referred as USC.

Actionable thresholds: CRAB features; same-day admission for MSCC or AKI; initial tests include FBC, ESR or plasma viscosity, renal function, and calcium, with serum immunoglobulins, serum protein electrophoresis, sFLC, or urine Bence Jones protein if suspicion persists.

#### NICE NG35: Myeloma diagnosis and management

URL: https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng35

Covers diagnosis and specialist management. The primary-care relevant recommendations are laboratory confirmation principles: use serum protein electrophoresis plus serum free light chains to confirm paraprotein, use immunofixation after abnormal electrophoresis, and do not use any single serum or urine paraprotein/light-chain test alone to exclude myeloma.

Actionable thresholds: no numeric primary-care referral threshold; the key threshold is abnormal serum protein electrophoresis requiring immunofixation.

#### NICE NG12: Suspected cancer recognition and referral

URL: https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng12

NG12 is relevant for primary-care suspicion and myeloma blood testing. The direct web extraction was broad and did not isolate the myeloma subsection cleanly, so the April 2025 myeloma update was cross-checked against NICE-linked Myeloma UK HCP summary and NG35 testing recommendations.

Actionable thresholds: age 60 years or over with persistent bone pain or unexplained fracture should prompt FBC, calcium, and ESR or plasma viscosity; suspicious inflammatory marker results should prompt urgent paraprotein/light-chain testing.

#### BSH/UKMS MGUS good practice paper

URL: https://b-s-h.org.uk/guidelines/guidelines/investigation-and-management-of-monoclonal-gammopathy-of-undermined-significance

Covers MGUS diagnosis, risk stratification, and monitoring. The landing page and HCP summary confirm the scope, but full paper PDF extraction was poor in this runtime. Thresholds are included with an editorial warning for local haematology confirmation.

Actionable thresholds used in this draft: low-risk MGUS as IgG paraprotein <15 g/L with normal sFLC ratio; refer or seek haematology assessment for IgG >15 g/L, IgA or IgM >10 g/L, sFLC ratio outside 0.1 to 7, or paraprotein rise >25% and >5 g/L.

#### Myeloma UK MGUS page and HCP material

URLs:

- https://www.myeloma.org.uk/understanding-myeloma/related-conditions/mgus/
- https://hcp.myeloma.org.uk/library/suspected-cancer-recognition-and-referral-ng12/

These sources support patient-facing MGUS explanation, progression risk, symptom safety-netting, and monitoring frequency. Myeloma UK describes MGUS as not cancer, usually asymptomatic, and monitored rather than treated. It lists common monitoring as every 3 to 4 months in the first year, then every 6 to 12 months if stable.

### Cross-source themes

- Myeloma suspicion should be driven by symptoms plus blood-test abnormalities, especially CRAB features.
- Same-day admission is needed for suspected malignant spinal cord compression or acute kidney injury.
- Raised paraprotein alone is not a USOC referral, but it may still need haematology advice depending on level, isotype, sFLC ratio, trend, and symptoms.
- MGUS monitoring intensity varies; local haematology confirmation is needed before publishing exact intervals.

### How themes inform pathway background

The pathway should reassure that MGUS is common and usually benign while making the myeloma safety thresholds explicit. The opening content therefore separates stable MGUS monitoring from suspected myeloma escalation.
