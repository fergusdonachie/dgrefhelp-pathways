## 1. Background (page contents — pre-accordion)

Dyspepsia is a clinical syndrome characterised by epigastric pain or discomfort, bloating, early satiety, nausea, or heartburn. It affects an estimated 25–40% of adults and accounts for a significant proportion of GP consultations.

**Key subtypes:**
- **Uninvestigated dyspepsia** — symptoms without prior endoscopy
- **GORD** — heartburn/regurgitation predominant, often without structural pathology
- **H. pylori-associated dyspepsia** — caused by or complicated by *Helicobacter pylori* infection
- **Functional (non-ulcer) dyspepsia** — persistent symptoms with negative investigations
- **Peptic ulcer disease** — gastric or duodenal ulcer, often H. pylori-related or NSAID-related

**What must not be missed:**
- Upper GI malignancy (gastric, oesophageal)
- Peptic ulcer with bleeding or perforation
- Cardiac disease mimicking dyspepsia

## 2. Primary care assessment

### History
- Duration, onset, character, and location of symptoms
- Relationship to food, posture, NSAIDs/aspirin, alcohol, smoking
- Previous H. pylori testing or eradication
- Previous endoscopy findings
- Weight loss (unintentional), dysphagia, odynophagia
- Family history of upper GI cancer
- Current medications: NSAIDs, aspirin, bisphosphonates, iron, SSRIs, calcium channel blockers

### Examination
- BMI and weight (document weight loss)
- Abdominal palpation: epigastric tenderness, mass
- Signs of anaemia (pallor, koilonychia)
- Lymphadenopathy (supraclavicular — Virchow's node)

### Red flags (require urgent action — see Who to Refer)
- Haematemesis or melaena
- Dysphagia or odynophagia
- Persistent vomiting
- Unintentional weight loss
- Epigastric mass on examination
- Iron-deficiency anaemia without explanation
- Age ≥55 with new-onset or unexplained dyspepsia

### H. pylori testing
- **Preferred test:** faecal antigen test (stool antigen test) — avoid serology (not validated for active infection)
- **Washout period:** stop PPI for ≥2 weeks before testing; stop antibiotics for ≥4 weeks before testing
- **Do not use** office-based serological tests
- Test before empirical PPI in uninvestigated dyspepsia (unless urgent OGD indicated)
- Confirm eradication: retest using urea breath test or stool antigen test at least 6–8 weeks after completing eradication therapy

### NSAID review
- If patient is taking NSAIDs: review indication and consider stopping or switching to lowest effective dose paracetamol-based analgesia
- If NSAID must continue: switch to a COX-2 selective agent plus a PPI

## 3. Primary care management

### Uninvestigated dyspepsia (no alarm features, age <55)
1. Test for H. pylori using faecal antigen test
2. If H. pylori positive → 7-day triple therapy eradication (see below)
3. If H. pylori negative or symptoms persist after eradication → empirical full-dose PPI for 4 weeks
4. Review response at 4–8 weeks; step down to lowest effective PPI dose or as-needed use
5. If symptoms recur on step-down: re-introduce PPI at lowest effective dose; annual review

### GORD (heartburn/regurgitation predominant)
- Full-dose PPI for 4–8 weeks
- Severe oesophagitis: full-dose PPI for 8 weeks; maintain at full dose long-term
- Step down to lowest effective dose once controlled
- Lifestyle: weight loss, head-of-bed elevation, avoid late meals, avoid triggers (fatty food, alcohol, caffeine, tobacco)

### H. pylori eradication therapy (NICE CG184 / SIGN guidance)

**First-line (7 days, all twice-daily):**
- Standard: PPI + amoxicillin 1g + (clarithromycin 500mg OR metronidazole 400mg)
- Penicillin-allergic: PPI + clarithromycin 500mg + metronidazole 400mg
- Prior clarithromycin exposure: PPI + bismuth subsalicylate + metronidazole + tetracycline

**Second-line (7 days, if first-line fails):**
- PPI + amoxicillin + whichever of clarithromycin/metronidazole was NOT used first-line
- Penicillin-allergic, no fluoroquinolone exposure: PPI + metronidazole + levofloxacin (note: fluoroquinolone restrictions apply — check current SMC/SIGN guidance)
- Penicillin-allergic with fluoroquinolone exposure: PPI + bismuth + metronidazole + tetracycline
- If exposed to both clarithromycin and metronidazole: PPI + amoxicillin + tetracycline (or levofloxacin if no contraindication)

**After second-line failure:** refer to gastroenterology; do not prescribe third-line empirically.

### NSAID-associated dyspepsia
- Stop NSAID where clinically possible
- If NSAID continuation required: use COX-2 selective agent at lowest effective dose + PPI
- Test for H. pylori and eradicate if positive
- Review NSAID indication every 6 months

### Lifestyle advice (all dyspepsia)
- Smoking cessation
- Alcohol moderation
- Weight loss if overweight
- Avoid trigger foods (fat, spice, citrus, caffeine)
- Eat smaller, more frequent meals; avoid eating within 3 hours of bedtime
- Raise head of bed (for GORD)

### Follow-up
- Review response at 4–8 weeks
- Gastric ulcer confirmed on OGD: repeat endoscopy 6–8 weeks after starting treatment (to confirm healing and exclude malignancy)
- Peptic ulcer with H. pylori: retest for eradication at 6–8 weeks post-treatment
- Long-term PPI users: annual review; encourage step-down or as-needed use

## 4. Who to refer

### Same-day / emergency (A&E / 999 if haemodynamically unstable)
- Haematemesis (vomiting blood)
- Melaena (black tarry stool)
- Signs of perforation (acute abdomen, surgical emergency)

### Urgent 2-week-wait OGD (suspected upper GI cancer — SACT/NG12 criteria)
Refer all patients aged ≥55 with ANY of the following unexplained symptoms:
- Weight loss + any of: upper abdominal pain, reflux, or dyspepsia
- Treatment-refractory dyspepsia
- Upper abdominal mass
- Dysphagia (any age — refer urgently)
- Iron-deficiency anaemia (investigate cause; if upper GI source suspected, refer)

Also consider urgent referral:
- Age <55 with persistent dyspepsia AND raised H. pylori serology AND family history of upper GI cancer (clinical judgement)

### Routine gastroenterology referral
- Symptoms unresponsive to adequate trial of H. pylori eradication AND PPI therapy
- H. pylori not eradicated after second-line therapy (for third-line/sensitivity testing)
- Patients considering anti-reflux surgery (laparoscopic fundoplication for GORD)
- Complicated Barrett's oesophagus (surveillance coordination)
- Diagnostic uncertainty where endoscopy needed but not urgent

---

## 5. Who not to refer

- Uncomplicated dyspepsia in patients aged <55 with no alarm features — manage in primary care
- H. pylori positive dyspepsia — treat and confirm eradication in primary care; refer only if eradication fails twice
- GORD well-controlled on PPI — continue in primary care with annual review and step-down
- Functional (non-ulcer) dyspepsia — once upper GI malignancy excluded (by age/alarm feature criteria), manage in primary care with PPI/lifestyle/low-dose TCA if needed
- Heartburn in pregnancy — manage with lifestyle and antacids; refer only if severe or atypical
- Dyspepsia in patients on NSAIDs where stopping the NSAID resolves symptoms
- Patients with known peptic ulcer who respond to H. pylori eradication (unless gastric ulcer requiring post-treatment endoscopy — arrange this locally)
