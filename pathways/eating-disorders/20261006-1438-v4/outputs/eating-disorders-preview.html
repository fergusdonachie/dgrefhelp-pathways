Eating disorders in adults

Eating disorders affect eating behaviour and mental and physical health. They include anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder and avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID). ARFID can involve sensory aversion, fear of choking/vomiting or little interest in food, without weight or shape concerns.

Patients may present indirectly with gastrointestinal symptoms, dizziness, menstrual disturbance, anxiety or poorly controlled diabetes. Serious illness can occur at any weight; normal BMI or blood tests do not exclude an eating disorder or physical risk.

The GP task: recognise a possible eating disorder, identify immediate risk, and refer for diagnosis and treatment. New suspected cases need specialist assessment even when physical risk is low.

Assessment

Recognise and ask

Consider an eating disorder with unexplained weight change, restricted eating, recurrent vomiting, excessive exercise, food/weight preoccupation or concern from family. Ask directly and sensitively:

  • Eating: What have you eaten and drunk recently? Do you avoid foods, skip meals or have episodes of eating with loss of control?

  • Weight and behaviours: Are you afraid of gaining weight? Do you vomit, use laxatives/diuretics, exercise to compensate, or omit insulin? How often?

  • Impact: How does eating affect daily life? Ask about mood, self-harm/suicide, alcohol/substances and support. Food avoidance without weight concerns may be ARFID.

A screening questionnaire can support the conversation but must not determine diagnosis or referral alone. Consider other causes of weight loss/vomiting, including diabetes, thyroid or gastrointestinal disease; investigate as indicated without delaying referral.

Initial physical assessment

Arrange face-to-face assessment; check previous weights/results before the appointment where available.

  • History: recent intake and weight trajectory; fainting, chest pain/palpitations, weakness, reduced urine output; purging, pregnancy and diabetes.

  • Examination: weight, height/BMI, pulse, lying/standing BP, temperature, hydration and muscle strength (sit up/rise from a squat only if safe).

  • Bloods: where restriction, weight loss, purging or physical symptoms suggest compromise, obtain FBC, U&Es/creatinine, LFTs, calcium, magnesium, phosphate and glucose. Add tests for the clinical picture/differential, e.g. TFTs or ketones. Check any specialist referral requirements.

  • ECG: rapid weight loss, significant purging, bradycardia, hypotension, weakness, excessive exercise, electrolyte disturbance or QT-affecting medication.

Do not delay emergency care or specialist referral to complete tests.

Emergency or same-day assessment

Call 999 for collapse with ongoing impairment, seizure, major haematemesis, suspected ketoacidosis with acute illness, or other immediately life-threatening presentation.

Any criterion below: arrange same-day acute medical assessment/discussion. Do not wait for several abnormalities. Involve mental health services alongside acute medicine when needed.

Concerning findings below the emergency thresholds

Contact CMHT/SEDS for urgent advice and agree the next review. Two or more of these findings, worsening observations or an unsafe home plan warrant same-day discussion/assessment:

  • Waking pulse 40–50 bpm; systolic BP <90 without symptoms, or a standing systolic fall >15 and ≤20 mmHg; temperature below 36°C without meeting the same-day thresholds.
  • BMI 13–14.9; loss 0.5–0.99 kg/week for 2 consecutive weeks when undernourished; restricted fluids/dehydration or noticeable difficulty sitting up/rising from a squat.
  • Vomiting/laxative misuse ≥3 times/week; excessive exercise >1 hour/day when malnourished; increasing restriction or inability to maintain intake.

These thresholds guide escalation, not admission or diagnosis. Deterioration, carer concerns, diabetes, pregnancy or inadequate support can increase urgency. Normal bloods and absence of these findings do not prove safety.

Primary care management

Before specialist assessment

Act on abnormal findings and refer according to urgency. Arrange further review where symptoms or findings require it; do not start a fixed eating-disorder monitoring schedule solely because a referral has been made.

If continuing physical surveillance is needed while waiting, seek an explicit interim plan with the receiving service: who provides it, reviews results and escalates deterioration. Unresolved clinical risk still needs action before diagnosis; escalate if a safe plan cannot be delivered.

  • Support: involve carers with consent, offer Beat, discourage excessive exercise and review purging and medicines that increase risk.

  • Safety-net: same-day help for fainting, worsening weakness, inability to maintain intake, palpitations or suicidal thoughts; 999 for collapse with ongoing impairment, seizure or immediate danger.

  • Nutrition: severe undernutrition, substantial weight loss, low potassium/phosphate/magnesium or little/no intake for over 5 days needs specialist advice on safe nutrition and refeeding risk. Do not delay necessary nutrition while arranging supervised care. New oedema, breathlessness or confusion after increasing intake needs same-day assessment.

After diagnosis — agreed primary care follow-up

Ongoing monitoring requires an agreed plan specifying observations/tests, frequency, result ownership and relapse/escalation triggers. This is separate from the initial assessment above.

For anorexia without ongoing specialist treatment, offer at least annual physical and mental health review; active illness may need closer review. Consider DEXA after 2 years of underweight, or earlier with bone pain/recurrent fractures. With vomiting, advise dental review, rinsing afterwards and avoiding immediate toothbrushing.

Who to refer

  • Emergency/same-day: use the action criteria in Assessment; medical instability needs acute medical care, with mental health input alongside it.

  • New suspected eating disorder: refer without delay for specialist assessment and treatment planning. A confirmed diagnosis or low BMI is not required.

  • Established disorder: seek specialist review for relapse, deterioration or an ineffective/unworkable care plan; telephone for urgent concerns.

Local route: the supplied ICP describes Community Mental Health Team (CMHT) assessment with onward Specialist Eating Disorder Service (SEDS) input. Referral method and urgent/out-of-hours contacts require local confirmation.

Include: presenting concerns/behaviours, intake, weight trend, observations, available dated bloods/ECG, physical and psychiatric risk, relevant medication/comorbidity and any interim arrangements. Forward outstanding results when available.

Under 18: CAMHS pathway. Pregnancy/postnatal: involve maternity/perinatal services. Diabetes: joint diabetes input; same-day advice for insulin omission, ketones or acute deterioration.

Who not to refer

Primary care without a new referral

  • Already assessed and diagnosed: stable intake, weight trend, observations and mental health; low current risk agreed with the specialist team; treatment needs addressed and a deliverable primary care follow-up plan.

  • After specialist discharge: continue the agreed relapse/follow-up plan while stable; re-refer if deterioration occurs.

  • No eating disorder suspected after initial assessment: investigate/manage the identified problem; reassess persistent or changing symptoms.

Low physical risk alone is not a reason to withhold a first referral. Specialist assessment establishes diagnosis and treatment needs; absence of emergency features does not establish safety.
