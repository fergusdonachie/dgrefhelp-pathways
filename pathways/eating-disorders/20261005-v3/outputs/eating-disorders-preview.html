Eating disorders in adults

Eating disorders are serious mental illnesses that can cause life-threatening physical complications at any weight. They include anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder (BED), other specified feeding or eating disorder (OSFED) and avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID).

Assess and refer early. Do not use BMI, appearance, illness duration, screening scores or normal blood results alone to decide whether someone has an eating disorder, how unwell they are or whether to refer.

Medical or psychiatric emergency

Arrange immediate or same-day emergency assessment for a MEED red flag, rapid deterioration, severe dehydration or malnutrition, significant biochemical or ECG abnormality, organ compromise, diabetic ketoacidosis, haematemesis, severe self-harm or high suicide risk.

This pathway is for adults aged 18 and over and supports primary care assessment, initial management and referral. Use the full Medical Emergencies in Eating Disorders (MEED) guidance and local emergency protocols when risk is identified.

Assessment

Recognise a possible eating disorder

Consider an eating disorder in people of any sex, gender, ethnicity or body size. Presentations may be indirect and weight may be low, within the expected range or high.

  • Restriction, fasting, avoidance of food groups, ritualised eating, binge eating, self-induced vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, insulin restriction, water loading, weight-control medicines, or excessive or driven exercise.
  • Rapid or unexplained weight change; marked concern about weight, shape or food; social withdrawal around eating.
  • Dizziness, fainting, weakness, palpitations, cold intolerance, gastrointestinal or menstrual/endocrine symptoms, dental erosion, parotid swelling, electrolyte disturbance or hypoglycaemia.
  • Depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, self-harm, suicidality, substance use, trauma, neurodivergence or marked functional decline.
  • Poorly controlled diabetes, recurrent diabetic ketoacidosis or unexplained insulin omission.

SCOFF

SCOFF can support initial questioning but is not diagnostic. Ask about self-induced Sickness, loss of Control over eating, loss of more than One stone in 3 months, feeling Fat despite others saying the person is too thin, and whether Food dominates life. Two or more positive answers raise suspicion, but a lower score does not exclude an eating disorder.

ARFID may involve sensory avoidance, fear of adverse consequences of eating, or low interest in food without weight or shape concerns. A negative SCOFF does not exclude this presentation.

History

  • Onset and course; current and previous weight; amount and rate of weight change; current intake; restriction, bingeing, vomiting, laxatives/diuretics, insulin or medication manipulation, fluid intake and exercise.
  • Duration of little or no intake; fainting, dizziness, weakness, chest pain, palpitations, breathlessness, cold intolerance, abdominal symptoms, haematemesis, seizures and cognitive change.
  • Depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, self-harm and suicide risk, safeguarding, capacity, social support and effect on daily functioning.
  • Diabetes, pregnancy/postnatal status, gastrointestinal or endocrine disease, renal/cardiac disease, osteoporosis/fracture, neurodivergence, alcohol/substance use and current medicines.

Examination

  • Weight, height and BMI (weight in kg divided by height in metres squared), interpreted alongside weight trajectory and rate of change.
  • Pulse, lying and standing blood pressure, temperature, respiratory rate, hydration, peripheral circulation and oedema.
  • Cardiovascular examination; muscle strength, including ability to sit up and rise from a squat; signs of vomiting, laxative misuse, malnutrition or another physical cause.
  • Mental state, self-harm/suicide risk and overall clinical concern.

Investigations

Do not delay urgent assessment or referral to complete investigations. Tailor tests to the presentation and urgency. Baseline assessment will commonly include FBC; urea, creatinine, electrolytes and bicarbonate; calcium, magnesium and phosphate; glucose; LFTs; albumin/total protein; and other tests such as amylase, CK, TFT, pregnancy test, ketones or venous blood gas when indicated.

Arrange a 12-lead ECG with rapid weight loss, severe purging, bradycardia, hypotension, weakness, electrolyte disturbance, excessive exercise, previous arrhythmia or medicines that may affect cardiac function.

Assess risk holistically. Do not use SCOFF, BMI, a single observation or normal laboratory results in isolation. Apply the MEED traffic-light framework and consider trajectory, behaviours, physical findings, psychiatric risk, capacity and available support.

Primary care management

Initial management

  • Explain the concern and the need for early specialist assessment. Agree how results and deterioration will be communicated.
  • Address immediate dehydration, hypoglycaemia, electrolyte or other medical problems according to severity and local acute protocols. Do not start an unsupervised refeeding regimen in a medically compromised patient.
  • Review medicines that prolong QT, lower blood pressure, disturb electrolytes, suppress appetite or increase sedation/falls risk. Ask specifically about laxatives, diuretics, weight-loss products, stimulants, insulin omission and non-prescribed supplements.
  • Involve family or carers where appropriate and with consent. Ask what reasonable adjustments would support examination, weighing and engagement; blind weighing may help some people.
  • Give a written safety plan: call 999 for collapse with ongoing impairment, seizure, severe chest pain or immediate danger; seek same-day help for fainting, inability to maintain food or fluids, marked weakness, palpitations, worsening purging or suicidal thoughts. Non-attendance may indicate increased risk and should prompt proactive follow-up and reassessment.

While awaiting assessment or during shared care

  • Individualise monitoring using MEED risk, current behaviours and rate of change. Do not assume the fixed intervals in the supplied LES or ICP are safe for a changing presentation.
  • Agree and document the next review date, named clinical lead, frequency of observations/bloods/ECG, who performs each task, who interprets and acts on results, and daytime/out-of-hours escalation contacts. A referral alone does not establish a transfer of responsibility.
  • Review sooner after any change in intake, purging, exercise, medication, weight trajectory, observations, bloods, ECG, mental state or engagement.
  • Primary care should not hold sole medical responsibility for a significant or severe eating disorder unless this has been explicitly agreed with specialist clinicians.
  • For anorexia nervosa in someone not receiving ongoing eating-disorder treatment, offer at least an annual physical and mental health review, escalating sooner according to clinical need.

If the proposed monitoring cannot be delivered, contact the specialist team promptly to agree an alternative setting or provider. On discharge or transition, obtain a written handover and relapse plan; avoid gaps in monitoring.

Physical health advice

  • Weight restoration and treatment of the eating disorder are the main measures for low bone mineral density. In adults with anorexia nervosa, consider DEXA after 2 years of underweight, or earlier with bone pain or recurrent fractures; do not arrange routine baseline DEXA solely because BMI is below 17.5.
  • With vomiting or laxative/diuretic misuse, assess hydration and electrolytes. Explain that laxatives and diuretics do not reduce calorie absorption. Encourage dental review; after vomiting, rinse with water or non-acid mouthwash and avoid brushing immediately.
  • Advise stopping excessive or driven exercise. Activity should reflect medical stability and individual risk rather than a BMI-only ladder; avoid high-impact activity where osteoporosis or fracture risk is present.

Specialist treatment should be person-centred and multidisciplinary. Depending on diagnosis, options include CBT-ED, MANTRA, SSCM and diagnosis-specific guided self-help. Ambivalence is a treatment need, not a reason to exclude or discharge someone.

Who to refer

Emergency or same-day assessment

Use the MEED traffic-light framework. One red factor or several amber factors should prompt urgent specialist discussion and a low threshold for acute assessment or admission.

  • Call 999: immediately life-threatening illness, collapse, severe cardiac compromise, seizure, gastrointestinal perforation/major haematemesis, or other emergency.
  • Same-day acute medical assessment: rapid deterioration; acute food/fluid refusal; severe dehydration; syncope; profound weakness; waking pulse below 40 beats/min; systolic BP below 90 mmHg with syncope or significant postural fall; tympanic temperature below 35.5°C (axillary below 35.0°C); significant ECG or biochemical abnormality; hypoglycaemia; diabetic ketoacidosis; uncontrolled purging or water loading; or other evidence of organ compromise.
  • Same-day mental health crisis assessment: severe self-harm, high suicide risk, or psychiatric/capacity concerns that prevent life-preserving care. Use the local Crisis Team/unscheduled-care pathway and involve acute medicine when there is physical instability.

MEED Table 1 red indicators also include adult BMI below 13 kg/m², acute food refusal or intake below 500 kcal/day for 2 or more days, and weight loss of at least 1 kg/week for 2 consecutive weeks in an undernourished patient. Rapid weight loss at any weight is concerning. No single BMI or weight threshold determines admission.

Biochemistry: the MEED frontline checklist flags potassium below 3.0 mmol/L, sodium below 130 mmol/L and glucose below 3.0 mmol/L for medical compromise. Obtain same-day acute advice/assessment; escalate immediately if unstable. Falling phosphate, other significant abnormalities or symptoms can require escalation before these thresholds. These are checklist triggers, not an exhaustive admission rule or the Table 1 red/amber boundaries.

Prompt specialist assessment

Refer suspected eating disorders promptly for specialist assessment, including apparently low physical risk presentations. The supplied ICP describes CMHT assessment and onward SEDS involvement. Local route to confirm: current referral destination, electronic route, urgent contact and whether direct professional/self-referral to SEDS is available.

  • For urgent concerns, seek direct discussion with the local CMHT/eating-disorder service using the current service directory; do not rely on an unacknowledged electronic referral.
  • The ICP describes CMHT assessment to determine the treatment plan and whether Specialist Eating Disorder Service (SEDS), dietetic, psychology, psychiatric or more intensive input is required.
  • If risk rises while awaiting assessment, contact the CMHT duty worker/specialist service and escalate the urgency. Use acute medical or crisis routes when indicated.
  • Under 18: use the local CAMHS pathway.
  • Pregnancy/postnatal: involve maternity/perinatal mental health services alongside the eating-disorder pathway.
  • Diabetes: arrange joint working with diabetes and eating-disorder/mental health clinicians; seek same-day diabetes/acute advice for insulin omission, ketones or acute deterioration; suspected ketoacidosis requires emergency medical assessment.

The ICP also describes dietetic referrals from GPs, CMHT, psychology and SEDS, with outpatient appointments at Mountainhall Treatment Centre and remote options. Confirm the current referral method. Dietetic input complements specialist eating-disorder assessment.

Include in the referral

  • Eating-disorder symptoms and behaviours, onset, duration, current intake, weight trajectory and rate of change.
  • Current weight, height and BMI, with pulse, lying/standing BP, temperature, hydration, muscle strength and other examination findings.
  • Blood results, ECG report and action already taken, with dates.
  • Vomiting, laxative/diuretic use, exercise, water loading, insulin/medicine manipulation and MEED risk factors.
  • Mental state, self-harm/suicide risk, capacity, safeguarding, substance use, comorbidity and current medicines.
  • Pregnancy/postnatal status, diabetes, neurodivergence, family/carer involvement, functional impact, previous treatment and adjustments needed for engagement.
Who not to refer

Do not withhold referral when an eating disorder is suspected. In particular, do not decline or delay referral because:

  • BMI is normal or high, weight loss has not crossed a fixed threshold, or the person appears physically well.
  • SCOFF is negative or blood tests are normal.
  • The illness appears mild, has been present for a short time, or the person is ambivalent about treatment.
  • Symptoms do not fit anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa; BED, OSFED and ARFID also require appropriate assessment.

Do not use routine CMHT referral as a substitute for:

  • Immediate or same-day acute medical care where the person is medically unstable.
  • The Crisis Team/unscheduled-care pathway for acute psychiatric risk.
  • CAMHS referral for people under 18.
  • Investigation or treatment of an alternative primary medical diagnosis when an eating disorder is not suspected.
