Eating disorders in adults

  • Unwell or deteriorating: assess for same-day medical/mental health care.
  • New suspected eating disorder: refer for assessment and a treatment plan, even if physical risk is low.
  • Established, stable disorder: primary care follow-up may be appropriate under an agreed plan.

Serious illness can occur at any weight. Normal BMI, bloods or a negative screening score do not exclude risk.

Assessment

At the consultation

  • Ask: recent intake and weight change; restriction, bingeing, vomiting, laxatives/diuretics, excessive exercise and insulin omission. Include food avoidance without weight/shape concerns (possible ARFID).
  • Check risk: fainting, weakness, chest pain/palpitations, dehydration, self-harm/suicide risk, pregnancy and diabetes.
  • Measure: weight, height/BMI and trajectory; pulse, lying/standing BP, temperature and hydration. Assess muscle weakness, including ability to sit up/rise from a squat where safe.

Initial investigations

  • Bloods: FBC, U&Es/creatinine, LFTs, calcium, magnesium, phosphate and glucose. Add TFTs, CK, amylase, ketones or other tests as indicated.
  • ECG: with rapid weight loss, purging, bradycardia, hypotension, weakness, excessive exercise, electrolyte disturbance or QT-affecting medication.

Use the MEED risk framework. Do not delay urgent assessment to complete tests.

Primary care management

While awaiting assessment or under an agreed follow-up plan

  • Arrange the next review: agree observations, bloods and ECG frequency with the specialist team according to risk and trajectory. Specify who reviews results and acts on them. Escalate if the required monitoring cannot be delivered.
  • At review: reassess intake, weight trend, purging, observations and mental health. Review sooner with change; follow up missed appointments.
  • Support: involve carers with consent; encourage regular eating with dietetic support, stop excessive exercise and review laxatives, diuretics and QT-affecting medicines. Significant malnutrition/refeeding risk needs a specialist-led nutritional plan.
  • Safety-net: same-day help for fainting, worsening weakness, inability to maintain intake, palpitations or suicidal thoughts; 999 for collapse with ongoing impairment, seizure or immediate danger.

Referral alone does not transfer responsibility. Significant/severe illness requires an explicit specialist agreement on medical responsibility.

Longer-term follow-up

  • For anorexia nervosa without ongoing specialist treatment, offer at least an annual physical and mental health review; active symptoms may need much closer monitoring.
  • Consider DEXA in adults with anorexia after 2 years of underweight, or earlier for bone pain/recurrent fractures.
  • With vomiting: dental review; rinse after vomiting and avoid brushing immediately. Offer Beat support.
Who to refer

Emergency or same-day assessment

999: immediately life-threatening illness. Same-day mental health crisis assessment: high suicide risk/severe self-harm; involve acute medicine if physically unstable.

Same-day acute medical discussion/assessment for any of the following:

  • Rapid deterioration, food/fluid refusal, severe dehydration, syncope, profound weakness, significant ECG abnormality or suspected ketoacidosis.
  • Waking pulse <40; systolic BP <90 with syncope/significant postural fall; temperature <35.5°C tympanic (<35.0°C axillary).
  • BMI <13; intake <500 kcal/day for 2+ days; loss ≥1 kg/week for 2 consecutive weeks in an undernourished patient. Rapid loss at any weight is concerning.
  • Potassium <3.0, sodium <130 or glucose <3.0 mmol/L; falling phosphate or other significant biochemical abnormality.

These are escalation prompts, not an exhaustive list or automatic admission criteria. Use clinical judgement and the full MEED framework; do not wait for several abnormalities.

Specialist assessment

  • New suspected disorder: refer without delay for assessment/treatment planning, including low physical risk presentations.
  • Established disorder: seek review for relapse, worsening intake/purging/weight trend, rising physical or psychiatric risk, or an ineffective/unworkable primary care plan. Telephone for urgent concerns.

Local route: the ICP describes CMHT assessment with onward SEDS input. Referral method and urgent/out-of-hours contacts require local confirmation.

Include: symptoms/intake, weight trend, observations, dated bloods/ECG, physical and psychiatric risk, relevant comorbidity/medication and current monitoring plan.

Under 18: CAMHS pathway. Pregnancy/postnatal: involve maternity/perinatal services. Diabetes: joint diabetes input; same-day advice for insulin omission, ketones or acute deterioration.

Who not to refer

Manage in primary care without a new referral

  • Established eating disorder already assessed: stable physical and mental health, low current risk agreed with the specialist team, and a documented plan for primary care follow-up. Monitoring must be deliverable and specialist treatment needs addressed.
  • After specialist discharge: continue the agreed monitoring/relapse plan while stable. Re-refer if the triggers above develop.
  • No eating disorder suspected after assessment: manage the identified problem and reassess if symptoms persist or change.

Low physical risk alone is not a reason to withhold a first referral. Initial assessment establishes diagnosis, treatment needs and whether primary care follow-up is appropriate. Absence of emergency thresholds does not by itself establish low risk.
